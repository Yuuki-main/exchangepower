The right battery is the one that fills up on most days and empties by morning. Too small and you still buy expensive evening power. Too big and you pay for capacity your solar cannot charge, especially in winter. Here is a simple way to size a battery for a WA home. For an overview of how batteries work, see [home solar batteries in Perth](/solar-battery/).

## Step 1: Find your evening and overnight use

Your battery needs to cover what you use from about 3pm to the next morning. If you have a smart meter, Synergy's My Account shows half-hourly data. Without it, a rough guide is that 50–60% of a typical household's usage happens outside solar hours.

## Step 2: Check your solar surplus

A battery charges from solar you would otherwise export. In winter, Perth panels make roughly half their summer output. If your system cannot fill the battery on a typical winter day after running the house, a bigger battery adds little.

| Solar system | Typical spare solar in winter* | Battery that usually fills |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [6.6kW](/residential-solar/6-6kw-solar-system-wa/) | About 8–12 kWh | 8–10 kWh |
| [10kW](/residential-solar/10kw-solar-system-wa/) | About 12–18 kWh | 10–15 kWh |
| [13.2kW](/residential-solar/13-2kw-solar-system/) | About 18–25 kWh | 13–20 kWh |

*Estimate after typical daytime use. Your installer should model your usage.

## Step 3: Use usable capacity, not nominal

Battery brochures list nominal and usable capacity. Usable is what you can actually draw. Rebates and the federal discount are also based on usable capacity. Compare quotes on usable kWh.

## Step 4: Factor in the rebates

- **WA Residential Battery Scheme:** $130 per usable kWh for Synergy customers (up to $1,300) or $380 per kWh for Horizon Power customers (up to $3,800). The rebate is capped at 10 kWh, so a 13.5 kWh battery receives the same state rebate as a 10 kWh one.
- **Federal Cheaper Home Batteries:** roughly 30% off through STCs for batteries of at least 5 kWh. Since 1 May 2026 support is tiered, with less per kWh above 14 kWh, and the rate steps down every six months.

Rebates should not decide your size on their own, but they make 10–14 kWh the best-supported range for most homes. See [WA residential battery rebates](/solar-battery/wa-residential-battery-rebates/) for the details.

## Worked examples

1. **Couple, 6.6kW solar, 9 kWh used after 3pm.** A 10 kWh battery covers most nights and fills on most winter days.
2. **Family of five, 10kW solar, 16 kWh used after 3pm.** A 13–15 kWh battery covers summer evenings fully and most winter evenings.
3. **All-electric home with EV, 13.2kW solar, 25 kWh after 3pm.** Consider 20 kWh+ or two batteries, knowing federal support per kWh is lower above 14 kWh.

## Power (kW) matters too

Capacity (kWh) is how much energy is stored. Power (kW) is how fast it can be delivered. If your evening load includes an oven, air conditioner and kettle at once, a 5 kW battery inverter may not cover it all, and the grid fills the gap. Check continuous and peak power ratings.

## Backup changes the sizing

If you want backup in a blackout, you also need to decide which circuits are backed up. Whole-home backup needs more power and capacity than essential circuits only. See [will a solar battery keep my power on in a blackout](/solar-battery-blackout-backup-perth/).

## VPP requirement in WA

To receive the WA state rebate, the battery must be on the approved product list and enrolled in an approved Virtual Power Plant. [Virtual Power Plants in WA](/virtual-power-plant-wa/) explains how that works.

## Quick sizing rule

Evening and overnight kWh × 1.1 = a good starting capacity, then check it against winter solar surplus. Already have solar? Read [adding a battery to existing solar](/add-battery-to-existing-solar-perth/), then [is a solar battery worth it in Perth](/is-solar-battery-worth-it-perth/).
