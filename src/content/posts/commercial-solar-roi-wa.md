*General information only, not financial or tax advice.*

Commercial solar often pays back faster than residential solar because businesses use most of their power during daylight hours. But the return depends on your load profile and tariff, not on a generic figure. Here is how to calculate it properly. For the sizes we install, see [commercial solar for WA businesses](/commercial-solar/).

## Step 1: Get your interval data

Ask your retailer for 12 months of 30-minute interval data. It shows when your business uses power, how much on weekends, and your peak demand. Without it, any ROI figure is a guess.

## Step 2: Match generation to your load

A solar system in Perth produces about 4.4 kWh per kW per day on average, peaking around midday. The more of that output your business uses on site, the better the return.

| Business type | Typical daytime share of use | Solar match |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cold storage, food processing | High, 7 days | Excellent |
| Manufacturing, workshops (weekday) | High on weekdays | Very good; weekend exports |
| Offices, medical centres | Moderate to high on weekdays | Good |
| Retail, hospitality | High, often 7 days | Very good |
| Schools | High on term weekdays | Good; holiday exports |

## Step 3: Value each kWh correctly

- **Self-consumed solar** saves your energy charge per kWh, from your bill.
- **Exported solar** earns a much lower buyback rate, or nothing on some business arrangements. Check yours with your retailer.
- **Demand charges** are based on your peak kW, often in the late afternoon. Solar alone may not reduce them much. A battery or load management can.

## Step 4: Account for the upfront discount and costs

Systems up to 100kW are eligible for STCs, which reduce the upfront price. Also budget for any switchboard upgrades, network application costs for larger systems, monitoring, and yearly maintenance.

## Step 5: Calculate

**Simple payback = Net installed cost ÷ (Annual bill savings + Export income − Maintenance)**

For a fuller picture, also calculate net present value and internal rate of return over 20–25 years, including panel degradation of about 0.4–0.5% a year and one inverter replacement.

## Worked example (illustrative)

| Input | Value |
| --- | --- |
| System size | [30kW](/commercial-solar/30kw-solar-system/) |
| Annual generation | About 48,000 kWh |
| Self-consumed | 75% (36,000 kWh) |
| Energy rate saved | 28c/kWh (example; use the rate on your bill) |
| Annual saving at 28c | About $10,080 |
| Export (12,000 kWh) | At your retailer's buyback rate |
| Maintenance | About $300–$500 a year |

At a net installed cost (after STCs) of around $30,000, simple payback in this example would be about three years. Your figures will differ.

## Network approval for larger systems

Since 1 May 2026, systems up to 30 kVA can connect under a standard connection. Larger commercial systems, such as a [50kW](/commercial-solar/50kw-solar-system/) or [100kW](/commercial-solar/100kw-solar-system/) system, need a separate application and technical assessment with Western Power, which can add time and may set export limits. Factor this into your timeline.

## Tax and accounting

Solar is a business asset and may be depreciable. Rules change, so speak to your accountant about depreciation, any small business write-off rules in force, and GST.
